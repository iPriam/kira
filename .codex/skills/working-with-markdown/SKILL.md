# Working with markdown

Write for a reader who is mid-task and will not read twice. Treat their
attention as the scarce thing and length as a claim on it that the content has
to justify.

## Front-load the finding

Lead with the conclusion, then support it. Never build toward a reveal — a
reader who stops after the opening paragraph should still leave holding the
point. Excise throat-clearing on sight: "In this document we will…", "It is
worth noting that…", "As mentioned above…" carry nothing a reader needs.

## Earn every paragraph

Weigh each paragraph against its own deletion, and keep only what changes what
the reader does or believes. Favor the sentence that carries a fact over the
sentence that frames one. Resist restating a heading in the line beneath it,
previewing what you are about to say, and narrating the document to whoever
reads it — either the content does the work or it does not, and describing it
never does.

Let length signal weight. A page about a small thing miscommunicates before a
word is read. Split a genuinely large topic instead of padding a short answer
to look thorough — and never crush a real argument into fragments to look
brisk. Concision means choosing what to omit, never compressing what stays.

## Fit the form to the content

Reach for a table only for short enumerable facts — a name, a path, a verdict
— and keep the explanation in the prose around it rather than inside a cell.
Reach for a list when order or membership is the point. Write prose everywhere
else: reasoning that spans two clauses belongs in a sentence, where the
connective tissue can do its job. Never chain arrows (`A → B → fails`) in
place of a clause, and never bury a dependency in a nested bullet.

## Prune on every pass

Delete before you qualify. When a claim needs a hedge to survive, the claim is
wrong — repair the claim. When an edit leaves a section echoing its neighbor,
cut one of them. Treat an unpruned document as unfinished rather than thorough.
